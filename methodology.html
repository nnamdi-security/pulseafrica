Polling methodology

How PulseAfrica collects, protects and publishes public opinion data, and what its figures can and can’t tell you.

Section 01

What is polling?

Public opinion polling is a systematic method of measuring the views, attitudes, and beliefs of a defined population by surveying a carefully selected sample of that population. Rather than asking every person in a country — which is both impractical — polling asks a smaller group and draws conclusions from their answers. How far those conclusions hold depends on how that group was chosen.

Population
The full group whose opinions we aim to measure. For PulseAfrica, this is all adult citizens of the United States of Africa — approximately 180 million people across 40 states and 450 tribes.
Sample
The smaller group who answer a poll. A random sample of 1,000–2,000 people can represent the views of millions. PulseAfrica’s samples are not random: they are the registered users who choose to answer each poll.
Bias
Any systematic error that causes a sample to over-represent or under-represent certain groups. PulseAfrica doesn’t correct for bias yet: results are raw counts, and the limitations below explain which groups are likely to be over- or under-represented.
Section 02

How polling works

Every poll on PulseAfrica goes through five stages, from question design to publication.

01

Question design

Questions are written to be neutral, unambiguous, and free from leading language. Each question is reviewed internally for balance and clarity before the poll is opened.

02

Who answers

Any registered user can answer any live poll. Nobody is selected or invited, so the people who answer choose themselves; they are not matched to the population by age, gender, state or education.

03

Data collection

Responses are collected through the online poll page from signed-in accounts. Each account can submit one response per poll, and the database refuses a second one.

04

Counting

Responses are counted as they are, without weighting. Figures count whole steps of 10 responses, so no single answer moves them.

05

Publication

Overall results appear once 10 people have answered. Breakdowns by group, and the trend over time, are published when the poll closes, leaving out any group with fewer than 10 responses. Every figure shows how many responses it is based on.

Section 03

Sample collection

PulseAfrica uses a voluntary opt-in panel of registered users. This differs from probability sampling: people decide for themselves whether to answer, and no stratification or weighting is applied, so results describe the people who answered rather than the whole population.

Section 04

Weighting (planned)

PulseAfrica doesn’t weight its results yet. Every figure is a raw, unweighted count of the responses received.

Weighting adjusts each response so that the people who answered match the population’s make-up by state, age, gender and other characteristics. It needs reliable population figures for each of those groups, which aren’t available yet.

Planned
Weighting will be introduced only once reliable population figures exist. It will be labelled on every result it affects, and the raw figures will stay available beside the weighted ones.
Section 05

Sample size and uncertainty

PulseAfrica doesn’t publish margins of error. A margin of error assumes a random sample, and PulseAfrica’s respondents choose to take part, so a margin calculated that way would make the results look more precise than they are.

How to read our figures
Every figure shows n, the number of responses it is based on. Figures based on fewer than 100 responses are marked as a small sample: at that size, a few people answering differently move the percentage noticeably. Groups with fewer than 10 responses aren’t published at all.

Breakdowns by state, age group or occupation rest on fewer responses than the overall figures, so they swing more. Treat differences between small groups with caution.

Section 06

Poll limitations

We believe transparency about what our polls cannot do is as important as explaining what they can. The following limitations apply to all PulseAfrica polls.

Online access bias
Our polls are conducted online. Citizens without reliable internet access are less likely to participate, which may under-represent rural and lower-income populations. Results are not weighted, so this isn’t corrected.
Voluntary response bias
People with strong opinions are more likely to seek out and complete opinion polls. This is a known limitation of opt-in panels, and PulseAfrica doesn’t correct for it yet.
Social desirability bias
Respondents may answer in ways they believe are socially acceptable rather than reflecting their true opinions. Careful question wording, and publishing answers only as totals, help mitigate this, but it cannot be fully eliminated.
Snapshot in time
Poll results reflect public opinion at a specific point in time. Opinions can shift rapidly in response to events. Each closed poll’s results page shows how answers changed while it was open, but a single poll result should not be treated as a permanent measure.
Section 07

What we don't do

Independence and integrity are the foundation of credible polling. These commitments are non-negotiable and permanent.

We don't accept poll commissions

No government body, political party, or private organisation can commission, fund, or direct a poll on this platform.

We publish all results

Results are published regardless of outcome. Unfavourable findings for any leader or party are never suppressed. The only things held back protect respondents: groups under 10, and breakdowns until a poll closes.

We don't share individual data

Individual responses are never shared with third parties, governments, advertisers, or any other organisation under any circumstances. Published breakdowns leave out any group with fewer than 10 responses, and a poll's percentages appear only once 10 people have answered.

We publish our methodology

Every results page shows how many responses each figure is based on and links to this methodology, which describes how every figure is calculated.

We don't interpret results politically

PulseAfrica publishes data, not commentary. We do not tell citizens what results mean politically or which leader they should prefer.

We correct our errors

If a methodological error is discovered after publication, we correct and republish with a clearly dated correction notice explaining what changed.

Section 08

Frequently asked questions

Can anyone participate in a poll?

Any registered adult citizen of the United States of Africa may participate. Registration asks for an email address, state of residence and age range; none of them is verified. Each account may submit one response per poll.

Are results updated in real time?

Response counts update as soon as people answer. Percentages move in steps of 10 responses, so no single answer changes them, and breakdowns by group are published when the poll closes. This protects respondents: nobody watching the results can tell how one person answered.

Who funds PulseAfrica?

PulseAfrica is an independent civic platform. It does not accept advertising, government funding, or political donations. Operational costs are covered through a transparent public interest grant model.

Why do I need to register to answer a poll?

Registration is required to prevent duplicate responses. Your answers are stored with your account so each person is counted once. They are never published, shown to other users or included in results except as totals.

How is PulseAfrica different from a government survey?

PulseAfrica is entirely independent. No government body has access to our data, can influence our questions, or can prevent publication of results. This independence is what makes the data credible and the platform useful.

How do I know results haven't been manipulated?

Every results page shows the number of responses behind each figure, and the breakdowns by group once the poll closes. This page explains exactly how the figures are calculated.

Satisfied with the methodology?

Your participation makes the data more representative for every citizen across all 40 states.
